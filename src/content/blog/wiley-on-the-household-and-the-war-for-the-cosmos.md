> *The Household and the War for the Cosmos: Recovering a Christian Vision for the Family*
>
> C. R. Wiley
>
> Moscow, ID: Canon Press, 2019.

Wiley sets the household in its cosmic frame. He begins by recovering the older meaning of piety, *pietas*: duty to God, family and country rather than private devotion. He traces it through the parallel stories of Aeneas and Abraham, whose rival claims to the world met in the first century. Reading Ephesians as a handbook for "guerrilla piety," he argues that the cosmos is an ordered, hierarchical whole, that the household is its first and natural community, and that fathers stand as its "middlemen." Households that keep the house code of Christ are the fulcrum on which the war for the cosmos turns.

## Piety

### The Deluge

Wiley shares that his interest in the household is rooted in the fact that he never really was a part of one until he married and started a family. He uses the experience of his upbringing to illustrate the cultural shifts in attitude toward social norms and the family. These shifts continue in our time, and "paradoxically, many of the other institutions in our society that once relied upon the household have turned against it" (6). Wiley outlines the cultural *status quo* that threatens the very existence of the household: marriage no longer is seen as a divinely-sanctioned institution but as a "lifestyle choice," which leads to all kinds of nonsense; children have lost value in society's eyes, and are seen as hindrances instead of blessings; the "single-person household," what once would have been an absurdity, is on the rise; there is a rapid slide toward socialism; and Christians are less and less able to think Christianly about the household. Wiley plans to address this problem in the book, beginning, surprisingly, with the notion of "piety."

### Whatever Became of Piety?

Wiley notes the disappearance of the word (and concept of) "piety" from our cultural vocabulary, and remembers when "piety" began to fall out of fashion. There was a movement away from the old-fashioned "piety" to "devotion," and then to "spiritual discipline"—more colloquially, the practice came to be referred to as "quiet time." The term "religion" has also received a downgrade in meaning and usage. The point is that words matter because they *mean* something. That is why the control of language in a culture translates into the control of thought. The downgrade of "piety" has reduced Christian faith to a "heart faith"—the heart is the only place that Jesus can be said to exist. Wiley uses the hymn "In the Garden" to demonstrate the power of this sentiment in popular religion. Then, he contrasts this with the ancient notion of *pietas* exhibited in stories such as Homer's *Iliad* and Virgil's *Aeneid*. *Pietas* carries the idea of *duty*: duty to God, family, and country; it has very little to do with privatized "religion." *Pietas* is closely related to the idea expressed by the Greek term *eusebeo* used in Acts 17:23 by Paul to commend the Athenians. In the ancient mind the world was a cosmos—a sacred, ordered, integrated whole, populated with beings who carried *de facto* responsibilities to recognize and operate within that ordered world. For them, there was no distinction between religious and non-religious spheres of life. On this view, the notion of "heart faith" is nonsense and impossible. Its interconnectivity also entails that politics and the family serve a necessary religious function in society, as foreign as that may sound to modern American ears. *Pietas* has debts to pay, and it undergirds the concept of *generations*.

### Aeneas, Abraham, and *Pietas*

Wiley does a comparative survey of the Bible and the *Aeneid*, noting along the way where their respective narratives intersect. Wiley sees a parallel between Abraham and Aeneas with respect to the nature of their calling, the most important aspect of which is that they will be *remembered*. Both characters also share a child of promise. Aeneas's son, Iulus, becomes the object/symbol of the future hope of Aeneas's fate as he seeks a promised land and a glorious future there. For Abraham, the promise is made while the prospect of children seems far fetched (Gen. 15:2–5). And this promise came with a covenantal sign (Gen. 15:9–18). Abraham is now, like Aeneas, duty-bound; he is now a pious man.

Another touching point is that Aeneas and Abraham both lead victorious "pious wars" against their enemies to possess a promised land. These men "felt a responsibility to establish a legacy" that would include many generations after them (38). And because both of these men were promised the world, then that necessarily entails that at some point their lines would meet and come into conflict—and they did. By the first century, Roman piety was expressed in the project of *Pax Romana*, which was used to justify their fierce imperialism. Four centuries later, Augustine critiques the ethics of *Pax Romana* as a whitewashed form of fratricide. Israel's failed project of securing and faithfully inhabiting their promised land spelled frustration for them by the first century, as they became an object of the *Pax Romana* themselves, and were now ruled by a puppet Roman king (Herod). Even though there was prosperity in the land, including the expansion of the temple, the pious Jews were outraged. Yet, "the hope of national sovereignty lived on" because of the prophecies of a Messiah that would usher in the Kingdom of God (44). Then tensions came to a head when Roman legions razed the temple in a decisive demonstration of authority and power. Prior to this, Jesus of Nazareth had been rejected by the Jews and crucified under Roman law, another sign of Roman domination against those who would threaten their preeminence. But in a quiet garden tomb, Jesus rose from the dead, and his followers were "radicalized," because even death they did not fear, nor could it squelch the coming Kingdom of God (45). This ushered in a new phase in the conflict between Abraham and Aeneas, as the methods of warfare could not be more different. The Christians won this war, as eventually, Rome submitted to another peace project, the *Pax Christi*. This was accomplished through what Wiley calls "guerrilla piety," of which the apostle Paul was the first to implement (46). The geographical scope of this new conflict was not just the Jewish homeland, it was the cosmos, itself.

## The War for the Cosmos

### Waging Peace

Paul's letter to the Ephesians is a "handbook for guerrilla piety" (51), Wiley claims—like the story of Aeneas, Paul's trajectory in this letter leads to warfare, a war for the cosmos. The meaning of the term "cosmos" is all but lost on us today, as we typically treat it as a synonym for "universe," but this is misguided. "Universe" denotes the singleness of reality, whereas "cosmos" denotes the order that underlies reality and gives order to all other things. This order was traditionally the result of an order-giver, a creator who endowed the world with such order, but that is not how "cosmos" is understood/used today. Carl Sagan's view has come to define the popular conception of the cosmos as "outer space," lacking hierarchy (etym: "sacred rule") and ultimate meaning (56). But in the ancient and Biblical view, the cosmos is layered, with higher, middle, and lower layers of existence, and we reside in the middle, what C. S. Lewis and J.R.R. Tolkien call "Middle Earth." And it is here that we get caught up in the tension of the "principalities and powers" who have insubordinated themselves against the proper order of the cosmos. Wiley reminds us that language plays a critical role in the cosmic war. Words have meaning, and meaning is embedded in reality. God's Word in creation (and redemption) is the source of the order (and re-ordering) of the cosmos.

In both the biblical and Roman conceptions of justice, the idea of equity is foundational: everyone—people and God—should get their due. The Romans had an elaborate social structure, and so "everyone was duty-bound in some way in this vast structure" (62). In the Biblical ethic, there is the duty to recognize what God has done, and return that blessing with gratitude in submission to His law. "Christian piety and Roman piety had the same basis in gratitude" (62). This caused problems for the Romans: if you owed the gods gratitude, but those gods were mutually exclusive deities claiming allegiance, whom do you pay? For the Christian, Jesus is not just a personal savior, He is the Lord of the Cosmos, and thus the ultimate object of allegiance. Augustus rose to power by defeating his rivals through military might within a weakened and dysfunctional Republic, and he was venerated for it, being seated in a heavenly place and ruling over a new household of peace. The apostle Paul plays on the irony of this in noting that the Lord Jesus gained his victory over rebellious authorities, has been seated in heavenly places, and now rules over His new household where former enemies (Jew and Gentile) are now friends.

### Lost and Found in the Cosmos

Modern political philosophy isolates individuals from their natural communities into which they are born. This artificial project was initially performed in order to understand why humans want to live in societies, but later became an instrument of liberation from the strictures of social institutions, like the family. Underneath this lies a set of philosophical assumptions that run contrary to a biblical (and ancient) understanding of humans and their place in the world. On this view, the world is ordered and hierarchical, like a set of Russian nesting dolls, where each doll lies embedded within its larger "world," with the same shape, but different size (cosmos and microcosm). And most importantly, this order is *natural*, not artificial. It is how God (or the gods) made the world. And among all the various nested microcosms, the family was understood to be the first community (in both priority and sequence) that humans experience.

Wiley now lays out his thesis explicitly: "household piety is as big as the cosmos" (70). But what is the household? A household is essentially an "authority structure," an "economy" (etym: "the law of the house") (70). The household is a place of work, not just recreation. The father plays a significant role in the vertical structure of this household economy, because hierarchy is necessary when serious work is to be done. Fathers are the "middlemen of the cosmos," standing between the household and heaven. He did not invent this role, but was placed in this position, and duty to its responsibilities is his work of piety. The modern family has outsourced its provision and authority, and the role of the father has been downgraded. The traditional view of the household has disintegrated because it has lost piety.

### House Stewards

Wiley homes in on the meaning of the term οἰκονομίαν, and laments that modern Bible translations have obscured its original sense in Ephesians 1:10, translating it as "administration" (NASB) or something similar (not even present in the ESV), which carries the contemporary connotation of leadership in corporate bureaucracies. In Ephesians 3:2, the NASB and ESV choose to translate οἰκονομίαν as "stewardship." This avoids the dangers of "administration," but "stewardship" is no longer associated with the household, so Wiley thinks the translation should make that connection explicit: "house steward."

In Ephesians, Paul uses the analogy of a household and a temple to talk about the church. Ironically, in chapters 2 and 3, Paul's discussion of the church as a reconciled household of Jew and Gentile sounds to modern ears like a politically correct solution to the contemporary issue of race relations, but to folks in his day, it would have sounded blasphemous. And, in chapters 5 and 6, Paul's discussion of the various relationships within the household is offensive to our cultural sensitivities, but likely was not for Paul in his day.[^1]

Wiley attempts to show that the household of God and our households are much more tightly connected than we typically think; they certainly were in the mind of Paul. There is a "house code" at work underneath the admonitions for the various members of the household; but this disturbs the modern sentiments regarding household order, particularly regarding the role of women. Wiley exposes the "hermeneutic of suspicion" that dominates scholarship today, wherein "things get squeezed to fit the categories of our time" in order to "uncover hidden agendas" instead of discerning the meaning of a text (85). He uses Maier's assessment of Xenophon as an example of this. He also exposes the anachronistic thinking that "read into the past things that people in the past could not have felt, or even thought, in their wildest fancies" (87).

Wiley posits that the best response to the hermeneutic of suspicion is to honor the dead, and recover a way of life that has been lost. By this, he means that we need to recover the household as a participant in the cosmic order of things. This requires a "man of the house" and a code by which he orders his household (89). Wiley attempts to show through an exegesis of Ephesians 3:14–15 that fathers are the axis between the cosmos and the household. He then surveys the economic and social dynamics at work in the ancient world to help the reader orient himself to the context into which Paul is writing regarding the instructions for slaves in Ephesians 6. This is the last of the instructions in Paul's house code, but Wiley begins with it because it helps explain the analogy of adopted heirs that Paul employs and the nature of the "obedience" that Paul commands of children—an obedience of loyalty and duty (piety).

### The Cosmos 2.0

Wiley now turns to the marriage relationship. This is the final point of crossing between the stories of Aeneas and Abraham. Both stories aim at a marriage, but in the Aeneas story, the wedding never occurs—Virgil dies before he can tell the end of the story. We are left with moral ambiguity as the last thing we read of Aeneas is that he kills his rival, but does not win the girl. The Aeneas story ends with death; not so with God's story. In a "marvelous paradox," the seed of Abraham falls to the ground and dies, but then becomes "very much alive" (103–104). That death and resurrection established a kingdom and won a queen, and the Bible's story ends with her cosmic wedding ceremony. Wiley reminds us that "the cosmos has a future," and it is on this foundation that our daily acts of piety are based.
Wiley has worked backwards within the household code in Ephesians, beginning with masters and slaves. He now addresses the relationship of husband and wife. This relationship is a "profound mystery," according to Paul. The issue of submission (Eph. 5:22) is offensive to our modern, liberated ears. This submission is rooted in the fact that the married woman is no longer her own; she belongs to another. That idea is scandalous, but it has not always been. Further, the offense assumes that the marriage relationship is simply a matter of the will, but this is not so—the relationship is based on belonging/possession, which is the only basis for hope. This same idea is evident in Paul's discussion of the church as the body of Christ in 1 Corinthians 12:12–31. The mystery of marriage is rooted in the doctrine of double imputation: all of our sin is imputed to Christ, and through his sacrifice of love, all of his life and grace and forgiveness is imputed to his bride, the church.[^2]

## Epilogue: Guerrilla Piety Made Plain

Wiley returns to his thesis that Ephesians is a "handbook for guerrilla piety," which contains a two-pronged strategy of fighting the cosmic war. The first strategy is understanding that there is such a thing as the household of God in the world—the church—and that this household promulgates itself in a certain way. The second strategy is our households, which are what Wiley calls the "fulcrum of the cosmos" (117). Our households participate in the cosmic war by following its code, which reflects the rule of Christ.[^3] Households have power because they are natural institutions, not artificial.[^4] Households are the crucible for production and fruitfulness (through both children and property), and thus are the means by which the kingdom of God will be promulgated on earth and the cosmic war won for Christ.

## My Remarks

Wiley is interested in setting the household in its *cosmic* frame of reference, precisely because this is what the Bible does. In doing so, he lifts the dignity and significance of the household to its proper place of respect and prominence. In fact, Wiley argues, the household is at the very center of the war for the cosmos, where principalities and powers collide. Contextualizing the household in this way bears ethical implications: Christian households and the churches they constitute must operate according to the house code of Christ. This is how salvation manifests itself corporately.

As the demise of the household continues, Wiley's call for a recovery of a Christian vision on this matter is welcomed. In pastoral ministry, I find that when working with families who are struggling in their life together, there is often a lack of a robust vision for the Christian household rooted in the mystery of marriage and the cosmic Lordship of Christ. Even speaking of the household in these terms sounds strange to Christians, because we have not been accustomed to thinking this way.

I was delighted to see some overlap between Wiley and Bavinck. I wish Wiley would have interacted more with the Hebrew Scriptures and weaved in its particular contribution to the NT's theology of the family. I thought his comparison of the Aeneas and Abraham stories was a clever rubric in which to explore the topic. This is a helpful and needed book, and it expanded my own vision for the Christian household in the war for the cosmos.
