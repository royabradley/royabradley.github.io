## Introduction

“Education” is a term that appears, on its veneer, to be quite benign. It does not immediately conjure up negative feelings or provoke fear when broached in a typical conversation. This is due, in part, to the vagueness and ambiguity of the term—“education” can and does mean many things to many people. The operative, cultural (“man-on-the-street”) conception of education may be articulated, more or less, as the formal, institutionalized process through which students go to receive credentials that afford them career opportunities later in life; matters pertaining to curriculum, pedagogy, culture, and religion are all treated as secondary to this main objective. On this view, education is pragmatic and provisional, not formative and holistic. To many, this conception is acceptable and uncontroversial, and would accurately describe their own educational outlook and experience. But what about the Christian? Do the Bible and the doctrines formulated out of its teaching inform and obligate the Christian to understand and approach education differently? Is there a moral duty for them to educate in a particularly *Christian* way, distinct from the *status quo*? I submit that Christian parents, in accordance with the support, guidance, and teaching of the church, have a moral responsibility to provide their children with, and maintain for themselves, a thoroughgoing, distinctively Christian education. I will argue that this imperative is entailed by the First Commandment (Ex. 20:3) by way of the classical Reformed approach of mining the Decalogue for ethical instruction in light of the rest of Scripture. I will also show how this imperative is consistent with and clarified by Paul’s exhortation for fathers to bring up their children in the *paideia* of the Lord (Eph. 6:4). I will then briefly trace the history of catechism in the church as a way of illustrating the consistency of conviction the church has held in regard to education for Christian life.

## The Ten Commandments: Narrow and Broad

It is customary in the Reformed tradition to regard the Ten Commandments[^1] as a summary of the Law, that is, a summary of what God requires of his moral creatures. In fact, according to many of the Reformed confessions and systematic theologies, the TCs function as the primary summary of God’s Law. Some might take issue with this perspective, and could argue that as far as summaries go, the Decalogue is certainly not the only summary of the Law found in Scripture, and also is not *necessarily* the best. After all, Jesus himself gave a summary of the Law, citing Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18, noting that “on these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets” (Matt. 22:40). In his summary, Jesus did not even mention the TCs. According to the New Testament, love is the central virtue in Christian ethics, but, interestingly, it is not identified as such in the TCs. Romans 13:8-10 teaches that love fulfills the law, and in John 13:34-35, Jesus teaches his disciples that love is a “new commandment.” In the most famous Bible passage on love, 1 Corinthians 13, Paul inextricably links love with good works. In this vein, love seems to be the best summary of the law and a sufficient standard for moral norms. So, why use the TCs as the primary summary over against these others? In response, I appeal to John Frame, who argues that the exegetical, historical, and theological uniqueness of the Decalogue supports its pride of place among the various summaries of the law in Scripture.[^2]

In addition to holding that the TCs are the primary summary of the Law, the Reformed tradition also understands the TCs to provide an expansive theological framework and basis for biblical ethics that has far-reaching implications, many of which are not explicit in the language of the commandments themselves. For an example of this Reformed approach, consider the Westminster Larger Catechism (WLC), which lays out hermeneutical guidelines to follow when attempting to interpret and appropriate the TCs. These guidelines reveal a hermeneutical logic that seems to stretch the TCs beyond their exegetical reach. However, whole-bible, intertextual reflection and a desire to apply these commands to one’s life will reveal that the authors of the WLC are attempting to do what Jesus, himself, does with the divine commandments—he understands them to be dealing with matters of the heart, and interprets them as such. As Frame says, “If we want to serve God, we will not only do what the commandments specifically say, but will also seek, with God’s help, the inner motives consistent with those commandments.”[^3]

One notable guideline that is outlined by the WLC is “That as, where a duty is commanded, the contrary sin is forbidden; and, where a sin is forbidden, the contrary duty is commanded; so, where a promise is annexed, the contrary threatening is included; and, where a threatening is annexed, the contrary promise is included” (Q. 99, Rule 4). Understood in this way, the TCs take on a remarkably flexible ethical-theological extensibility. As they come to us in the Decalogue itself, the TCs are stated negatively and narrowly, but may, under the rubric and method outlined in WLC, be shown to entail positive and broad ethical imperatives (such as “That one and the same thing, in divers [sic] respects, is required or forbidden in several commandments” [Q. 99, Rule 3]). This perspective on the TCs helps us understand how the first commandment entails a duty to educate.

## Grace and Law

“I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery” (Ex. 20:2). Yahweh addresses the assembly here in a way that identifies them as a liberated people; a distinction is made between their former status as slaves in Egypt and their new status as Yahweh’s elect—liberated and now safe by the mighty power of his merciful hand. Because of Yahweh’s gracious deliverance, they now bear the responsibilities and enjoy the privileges of being in covenant with the one true and living God. This text is a powerful witness to the grand theological theme of the relationship between grace and law. “It was the God of salvation who imposed his law on his people; the grace that saves preceded the law that demands. The people were given the law not in order that they might become the redeemed, rather it was because they had already been redeemed that they were given the law.”[^4] The word “law” has negative connotations in English, and this obscures the positive nature of the Law as it is revealed in Scripture. But it must be noted that the Hebrew <span lang="he" dir="rtl">תּוֹרָה</span> (*tôrâ*) is derived from <span lang="he" dir="rtl">יָרָה</span> (*yārâ*) which means “to teach” or “to instruct.” The sense here is one of a careful and conscientious parent who instructs their beloved child in the way of wisdom and life for their own wellbeing. This sentiment is echoed in Deuteronomy 4:1a: “And now, O Israel, listen to the statutes and the rules that I am teaching you, and do them, that you may live…” This proper understanding of the harmonious relationship between grace and law sets the tone for embracing the TCs as God’s gracious gift of instruction in righteousness before Him, and plays heavily into the First Commandment’s implications for education, for the law itself is an education from the LORD.

## The First Commandment and Education

“You shall have no other gods before me” (Ex. 20:3). The First Commandment prohibits the worship of any other God than Yahweh, the covenant God of Israel. This commandment assumes that there are rival gods, and makes a clear distinction between Yahweh and those other gods. This distinction is grounded in the nature of truth, itself. Thus, the First Commandment entails the responsibility to tell the truth, particularly the truth about God. And since the natural way to discover truth is through the teaching-learning process, education emerges out of the First Commandment as a moral duty.

## Antithesis and the Challenge of Secularism

To grasp this more fully, it is necessary to recognize the antithesis that exists between the believing mind and the unbelieving mind, and the threat of idolatry that secularism poses the Christian church. “The principles by which believers live are squarely opposed to the principles by which unbelievers live. This is true in the field of education as well as in the church.”[^5] The challenge of secularism is ubiquitous in our day, and it pervasively threatens the church from every direction. A worldview that does not recognize the lordship of Jesus Christ will inevitably approach every aspect of life from a different set of presuppositions, many of which are false. What is more, secularism, especially in education, has taken pride of place among competing views. Frame notes correctly that “The privileged position of secularism is a manifest injustice,” and this posture of dominance “provides the greatest challenge to Christians who seek to live by the First Commandment.”[^6] The antithesis that exists between secularism and Christianity in education affects everything. “Christian teachers know that not a single ‘fact’ can really be known and therefore really be taught unless placed under the light of the revelation from God. Even the laws of arithmetic cannot be known otherwise.”[^7] So, we must educate as Christians or as non-Christians. The choice is stark, and the illusion of “neutral ground” can be “pretended in education, but not accomplished.”[^8] Education is the process of learning to fight against idolatry and to serve wholeheartedly one’s God. The Christian God is the God of truth, and in submitting and conforming to his truth, we rebel against all falsehood. “All denials of this antithesis are therefore epistemological camouflage... In a war, what better strategy to use than to convince your enemy that there is no war?”[^9] Though not widely recognized, there is a war raging in education for the souls of Christian children (and adults). Therefore, Christians must approach the educational task with this squarely in mind, and recognize that there is “right” and “wrong” *everywhere*, not just *somewhere* (moralism). “If everything in the universe is dependent upon Yahweh, the sovereign creator, then nothing should be interpreted independently from him.”[^10] There is no neutral ground in education.

## Education is for Christian Life

My contention is that Christians bear the responsibility to educate, and more specifically, that the nature of this education should be thoroughgoing and distinctively Christian, and must reckon with the antithesis that exists between belief and unbelief. This claim now deserves further definition: by “thoroughgoing and distinctively Christian,” I mean to communicate that the goal of this educational endeavor is to form human persons toward faithful and fruitful Christian living. With Nicholas Wolterstorff, I agree that

> The curriculum of a Christian education is for Christian life. It is not for the training of the theological sophisticates, not for the continuation of the evangelical churches, not for the preservation of Christian enclaves, not for getting to heaven, not for service to the state, not for defeating the communists, not for preserving the United States or Canada, not for life adjustment, not for cultivating the life of the mind, not for producing learned and cultured people. Christian education is for Christian life.[^11]

This goal may be accomplished through a variety of ways and means, and I recognize that there is debate among Christians regarding the particulars of how best to go about the project of education. There is certainly some room for liberty in the matter, but I do not condone an approach that fails to reckon with the unique demands of a truly Christian education, which will necessarily entail certain distinctives in both method (pedagogy) and content (curriculum).[^12] Because they worship the one true and living God and no other—I AM, the God of truth—Christians have a duty to approach the educational task with great care and intensive labor.

## *Paideia*

The importance of this responsibility is picked up and clarified by Paul in Ephesians 6:4: “Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction [παιδεία (*paideia*)] of the Lord.” Because of his influence and position of authority in the government of the household, the father has the capacity to do great harm or good in the lives of his children. A father who abuses his office may certainly provoke his children to anger. But the Christian father, according to Paul, has the responsibility not only to avoid the provocation of anger, but instead, in a positive way, “discipline and instruct” his children. This is a familiar and oft-quoted verse, but we must recognize that “Paul’s requirement here is actually one of the most far-reaching commands of the New Testament.”[^13] This is because the concept of *paideia* “goes far beyond the scope and sequence of what we call formal education.”[^14] *Paideia* denotes “the act of providing guidance for responsible living”[^15] (hence the translation “discipline and instruct”), which in the ancient Greek conception involved nothing less than the formation of the ideal man according to the virtues and values of Greek culture. It was a grand task of enculturation, holistic in nature and broad in scope.[^16] For Paul, then, Christian fathers ought to be about the work of providing the “paideia of the Lord” to their children—giving them a full-orbed vision of the Christian life and how that vision is to be lived out in the world to the glory of God. Paul, as usual, roots this command in the doctrinal instruction he gives in the first three chapters of Ephesians. In this way, the responsibilities and maintenance of the Christian household are grounded in the manifestation of God’s grace in the redemption wrought by Jesus Christ. It is notable that this is the same pattern Yahweh employed in his prologue to the TCs, grounding his moral commands on the truth of his gracious redemption.

## Catechesis

One of the ways that the people of God have accomplished this task of education (in the sense I have already stipulated) is through catechesis. Etymologically, catechism simply means “to sound down” in anticipation of a reply (echo).[^17] Catechism is most often understood in a narrow sense to indicate that particular method of instruction that involves a question-answer format, and, traditionally, Christian catechisms have been tightly linked with a particular creedal affirmation, such as the Westminster Standards. But catechism may also be construed more broadly to indicate any form of instruction sanctioned by the church for the purpose of dogmatic and religious education. Since the time of Moses forward, “the primary purpose of education in Bible times was to train the whole person for lifelong, obedient service in the knowledge of God (Prov. 1:7; Eccles. 12:13).”[^18] Scripture, itself, directly teaches and gives examples of catechesis, broadly construed. The book of Proverbs, for example, on its own admission, is entirely devoted to the instruction of godly wisdom in the fear of the LORD (1:2-7).[^19] Throughout history—from the *Shema* of Moses (Deut. 6), to the Jewish synagogues, to the teaching ministry of Christ, to the apostolic message and method carried forward in the early church, through the catechisms of the Medieval church and the correction of errors made by the Protestant Reformation, to the Mayflower Pilgrims who settled in their own “promised land” with a catechism, Psalter, and Bible in hand—the people of God have understood that the task of education is of great importance and necessary for the work of Christian discipleship and cultural transformation.

## Conclusion

I have labored to show that the First Commandment entails a responsibility for the church, and in particular, Christian parents, to educate children for faithful Christian living. Furthermore, I have shown that this is not merely an “OT command” relegated to the past that is subject to New Covenant alteration, but rather that the New Testament reinforces this idea, most explicitly in Paul’s injunction to Christian fathers in Ephesians 6:4. The task, primarily through the ministry of Christian parents, of providing a Christian education for faithful Christian living is not optional. In fact, “the education of children for God is the most important business done on earth.”[^20]
