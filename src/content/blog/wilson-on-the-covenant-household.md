> *The Covenant Household*
>
> Douglas Wilson
>
> Moscow, ID: Canon Press, 2022.

Wilson argues that the household is formed by covenant. Human beings are bound together, not atomistic, and covenant headship, seen in Adam and in Christ, is the pattern for marriage: the husband takes responsibility for his household as Christ took responsibility for his bride, and love of that kind makes its object lovely. Wilson catalogs the common sins of husbands and wives, sketches the godly husband and the godly wife, and extends the covenant to the family as a whole. Authority flows to those who take responsibility, and parents raise their children not by striving but by trusting God's covenant promises in faith.

## The Meaning of Federal Marriage

Individual humans are not atomistic, but are interconnected ("*inter*-dividuals"). Wilson says we should think of ourselves, not as ball-bearings in a box, but as leaves on a tree. Our interconnectedness is held together by covenant. The term "federal" originally meant "covenantal." Central to this idea is the notion of headship (1 Cor. 11:3; Eph. 5:22–33). The Bible relates marriage to a covenant (Prov. 2:16–17; Mal. 2:13–16)—"where there is no covenant, there is no marriage" (7). There are two examples of federal headship in the Bible: Adam and Jesus Christ. When Adam sinned, we sinned, because we sinned *in* Adam. But the same is true of Jesus: when Jesus died, we died, and the penalty for our sin was paid by that death, because Jesus is our federal head. Wilson notes that "to believe this, you need a different paradigm for what the human race actually is" (8). The Bible calls Jesus a second Adam (some prefer "last Adam") in 1 Corinthians and Romans. Every human is either represented by the first Adam, whose sin brought us under a curse, or the second Adam, whose obedience rescued us from that curse. This covenantal association is at the heart of the gospel, it is the instrument by which we can be saved. "Christ's blood was the blood of the new *covenant*" (10).

In marriage, headship in husbandry means imitating Christ in his role as covenantal head. This entails that husbands should take full responsibility for the actions of their wives and children, just as Jesus took responsibility for sins he did not commit. Women are culpable for their own sins before God, but as wives, they are not responsible for the sins of the family in the same way the husband is. Within the ethics of the covenant, "love bestows loveliness" (14). Love that takes responsibility secures the loveliness of the beloved (Eph. 5:26–27). A husband's love for his wife should be efficacious, transforming her. It should also be incarnational: as Jesus' love for his bride was expressed in his willingness to die for her, so too a husband's love must be expressed in the form of death to self and sacrifice to the other. He who loves his wife, loves himself, and the covenant husband will soon realize that his love will be returned with interest—"Godly wives are multipliers" (18).

## Common Sins in Marriage

Wilson addresses seven sins that husbands tend to commit and seven sins that wives tend to commit. The first sin that a man tends to commit in his marriage is the sin of forgetting that he is responsible for all the sin in his household—the abdication of their federal responsibility. Second, men tend to make excuses for themselves instead of taking "masculine initiative, authority, and glad acceptance of sacrificial responsibility" (22). Third, men are often unfaithful to their wives in their sexual lusts, which Jesus forbids (Matt. 5:27–28). Fourth, men have a tendency to express harsh bitterness toward their wives (contra Col. 3:19) for what their wives have done or not done to them. Fifth, men tend to be blockheaded in their marriage, failing to live with their wives "with understanding" (1 Pet. 3:7). Sixth, men can sin in their marriage by not providing for the provisional needs of the household. And seventh, men can be lazy, which violates the clear biblical ethic of work.

Women tend to sin in marriage by, first, being disrespectful toward their husband, which violates her biblical duty (Eph. 5:33).[^1] Second, a woman can tend to nurse resentment against her husband in a way that causes bitterness, which is often more sinful than the sins of the husband that caused the resentment in the first place. Third, women tend to have a spirit of accusation against their husbands (à la Job's wife), blaming them for calamities they have no control over instead of ministering to them in light of the situation. Fourth, women tend to have the "little helper syndrome," which confuses being a *help* to the husband versus being the Holy Spirit's little helper. Fifth, a woman tends to fail in recognizing her role in providing sexual protection for her husband.[^2] Sixth, women can exhibit a false submissiveness by wanting their husbands to *look* like they are leading without actually making decisions the wife disagrees with. Submission comes into play when the woman does not get her way and still gets happy about it. And seventh, women can be "leaky faucets" (Prov. 19:13) by simply not letting things go and insisting that their complaints continue to be heard. This drives a wedge in the marriage.

## The Sketch of a Godly Husband

A man must first love Jesus Christ above anything or anyone (Luke 14:26), and so as a husband, he must be "completely dedicated to the glory of God through Jesus Christ" (39). In other words, a Christian husband must be a *Christian*. Secondly, a godly husband must be a man, not a boy in terms of the quality of masculinity, not age, which entails the readiness to accept manly responsibility (Wilson comments on the phrase "man up" [41]). Third, a godly husband should have a sense of purpose, direction, and calling for his life. Fourth, a godly husband should know how to exercise Christ-like leadership.[^3] Fifth, a godly husband is monogamous—"God's pattern is one woman, one time" (46). Sixth, a godly husband is tribal, thinking in terms of his ancestors and his descendants. He should understand his legacy to be more than economic, a legacy of faithfulness and a good name, which means he must teach his children after him to follow in the way of the Lord. Seventh, a godly husband is industrious, not lazy. Eighth, a godly husband provides food and clothing from the work of his hands. Ninth, a man should be courageous, especially in providing a wall around his wife, protecting and defending her against insults and threats.

## The Sketch of a Godly Wife

A godly woman respects her husband as she is commanded to do (Eph. 5:33), showing respect even when the husband is not deserving of it. A godly woman also manages the home well, for she is the "house-despot" (Titus 2:3–5; cf. Prov. 31:27). A woman's priority should be the home. A godly woman is in command of her tongue (Prov. 31:26). A godly woman is sexually responsive (Song 7:1). A godly woman shops wisely (Prov. 31:14).[^4] A godly woman is to be theologically educated (1 Tim. 2:11). "Women are as much a part of the intellectual arm of the Christian faith as are the men" (70). A godly woman respects masculine leadership (1 Cor. 14:34–35), but submission does not equate with ignorance. A godly woman is involved in the mission of the Church just as much as men (Phil. 4:3). A godly woman dresses well and modestly and glorifies the church in her loveliness. Her appearance is a sermon on the condition of her marriage and her household, for she is the crowning glory of creation (1 Cor. 11:5–10).

## The Federal Family

A family is a covenantal entity because it is the product of marriage, which is also a covenantal entity. A covenant entails responsibility to one another, but we are not accustomed to thinking about families this way. The state prefers an atomistic society that is void of covenantal connections because individuals are easier to control than tight-knit covenantal communities, like the family and the church. Covenantal connections are the fabric of reality, and the most foundational covenant in human terms is marriage. When marriages go awry, the husband, as the federal head, should take responsibility for it—"authority flows to those who take responsibility" (80). But this authority is not domineering, but kind. When this kind of authority is exercised, it begets a pattern of responsibility downstream. Jesus taking responsibility by suffering for our sake only produces a greater reason for us to take responsibility for our sins, not less. In this way of thinking, the family is an indivisible unity, wherein each member has both individual and corporate responsibilities. The most important event in history centers around the God-man, Jesus, who took responsibility for that which he did not do, and so sets the covenantal pattern for us to follow. When the head of the family makes a decision, the family makes a decision. But when the head wants to go north, and the body south, a problem arises. The possibility of this problem leads many heads to be apprehensive in their headship. In this case, the head should devote himself to prayer on the matter, and seek God's will in how he should lead his family, and the spirit of this prayer should be concrete, even asking God to upset things in order to test and provide opportunities to overcome them as an instrument of growth and maturity. This is radical discipleship.

## Common Sins of the Household

The tendency exists to fight the corruption of our age with laws and rules, but these by themselves do not deal with the real issue, which is sin. We need to discover the root problems so we can begin addressing them with the grace of the gospel.

Wilson says that parents should take care not to neglect the state of their own souls, for one cannot give what he himself does not have. "Those who do not know the nature of their own souls are in no position to shepherd others" (97). Parents must also not adopt a position of "defensive isolationism" (98)—the family's life is lived within the life of the church. There is a false assumption among many parents that they know their children better than anyone else in the Church. These parents need the reminder that "the wounds of a friend are faithful, while the kisses of an enemy are deceitful" (Prov. 27:6). Parents can be "played" by their children, and sometimes need the objectivity of a loving friend to point that out, for example. Furthermore, Wilson says that parents should avoid "ignorant isolation," comparing oneself to only a small, selected sect (cf. 2 Cor. 10:12) (102). Wilson notes that this is often the plight of homeschooling families. On the other hand, for those with kids in a Christian school with traditional classrooms, there is a tendency to sinfully presume that responsibility relies entirely upon the community at the expense of parental responsibility.

## Sketch of Godly Parents

Godly parenting, like living the Christian life in general, can only be done by faith, not by works.[^5] We receive the gracious promise of God to keep covenant to a thousand generations, and then live by that promise in our parenting. This does not preclude good works. But we *labor* in faith, trusting in God's promise, not our works.[^6] The covenant promises are to our children and grandchildren, which we did not earn, but were given and entrusted to us. This means we should receive them as a blessing, freely given. Being *in* the covenant is not enough; we must *keep* covenant with the Lord. This is covenant maintenance accomplished by faith. We have a duty to simply believe the promises of God. Real faith is constant, not prone to fits and starts. Though God saves people in all kinds of ways, through different circumstances, and at different times, the covenant nurture is the ordinary way God does this. When the question of God's sovereignty arises with respect to the election of covenant children, Wilson says we must distinguish between doubts and questions: "questions have answers, doubts never do" (120). The question of sovereignty that expresses doubt regarding the election of covenant children should be avoided, since covenant parents believe on God's promises, which are sure.

## Worldview Parenting

Christian parents must take the world and the "world system" (1 Jn. 2:15–16) into account in their parenting (123). Wilson distinguishes between things that are *worldly* and things that are *earthy*. The world contains physical, material pleasures, which are good; *earthy*, not *worldly*. But other things, like books of sorcery, are *worldly*, and should be identified as dangerous. Parents must train their children to take a stand against worldliness and its idolatries. This doesn't mean that, say, in evaluating whether to let your children watch a movie, that you simply ask if it has cursing in it, but that you consider what worldview messages it is communicating that may be seductive and harmful. As parents deal with media and music, Wilson says they should engage with wisdom by avoiding [1] dumb distractions, [2] legalism (i.e., have a better reason for saying 'no' than "because I said so"), [3] ignorance (i.e., making blind decisions), [4] sanitized imitations, and [5] teach your children through your own experience with music and media, making it a part of the life of your family, talking about it together and sharing in it together. Christian parents should beware the prevalence of aesthetic relativism that dominates contemporary evangelicalism and seek to instill a sense of the objectivity of beauty in their children. In all this we must keep covenant by faith. "This means that we work out the good works that God has prepared beforehand for us to do, and we do this knowing that He is at work in us to will and to do" (137).
