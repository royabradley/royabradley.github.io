## Introduction

In the well-known musical, *Fiddler on the Roof*, the main character, Tevye—a poor, lively milk-man living with his family in a Jewish community in 1910 Russia—fantasizes through song what life would be like if he were to be rich. At one point near the end of his signature solo, “If I Were a Rich Man,” the mood breaks, and Tevye speaks these words in prayer to God: “Lord, Who made the lion and the lamb, / You decreed I should be what I am. // Would it spoil some vast, eternal plan / If I were a wealthy man?”[^1] Beneath the whimsy of this lyric lies the conviction that God has indeed “decreed” that Tevye be what he is in accordance with “some vast eternal plan.” God is, in Tevye’s mind, superintending the created order according to his divine purposes, which is one of the core tenets of the Christian (and Jewish) doctrine of divine providence (hereafter referred to as DDP). But what, exactly, is DDP in its doctrinal formulation? What problems arise from saying that God is provident over his creation? What practical value does this doctrine afford the Christian? Much in every way! In its fullest expression, DDP is the bedrock of Christian hope and confidence in the face of evil and suffering, and thus, it is essential to the practice of pastoral care in shepherding souls through difficult trials that mock faith and challenge trust in a sovereign God. This paper will outline a broad notion of divine providence and attempt to articulate DDP in summary form. Two ethical-theological problems that arise from DDP will be addressed, and finally, the application of DDP to the practice of pastoral care will be explored.

## The Doctrine of Divine Providence

Throughout the history of the Christian church, believers have held that God is in some sense provident—that he is providing for and guiding his creation, including the evil that befalls it, to a purposeful end. This is not at all surprising, since Scripture’s witness to God’s providence is utterly clear, and creation itself testifies to this reality (Ps. 104:24). “The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord” (Prov. 16:33). God is directing the entire course of history, and in some sense, is in control of every minute detail of creation’s existence through time. More particularly, his providence applies to the affairs of human beings. One of the ways the Old Testament articulates this in regard to the providential care of God’s covenant people is through a kind of “cultic confession,” repeatedly acclaiming that God exercises steadfast love and covenant mercy to his people,[^2] and that he will lead his creation, through them, to a glorious end (Jer. 29:11-14). The New Testament reiterates this theme of God’s meticulous care for the world (Matt. 6:26-30), and teaches that all the minute details under his sovereign care are being drawn together into a mosaic that will, in the end, display the beauty of divine wisdom (Rom. 8:28-39), giving the Christian great confidence and deep meaning in life (Matt. 6:8; 7:7-8).

Like most titles of systematic doctrines, the term “providence” is not explicitly used in scripture to designate DDP. Its etymology comes from the Greek noun, πρόνοια (*pronoia*, thoughtful planning to meet a need; forethought, foresight, providence),[^3] through the Latin noun *providentia* (foresight, forethought), and the related verb, *providere* (to provide for, take precautions for or against).[^4] Thus, the term itself is quite instructive in giving us a good sense of what divine providence means in general.

Because systematic theology hangs together as a matrix of interconnected truths, DDP cannot be isolated from other aspects of the doctrine of God. One pertinent example of this is evident in how God’s *perfection* entails his *providence*, or to put it the other way around, how his *providence* rests on his *perfection*. Because God is absolutely perfect in every way, we can immediately infer that he is perfect in his knowledge, power, and goodness—that is, he is omniscient, omnipotent, and omnibenevolent. With this as an operating premise, Thomas P. Flint formulates a definition of DDP in the following way: “The notion of divine providence that orthodox Christians have typically come to endorse…is essentially a picture of how a God who is perfect in knowledge, love, and power exhibits those perfections through the detailed control he exercises over his creation.”[^5] Flint goes on to say that this traditional picture of God sees Him as “knowingly and lovingly directing each and every event involving each and every creature toward the ends he has ordained for them.”[^6] This concept of providence is consistent with the classic Reformed teaching that is based on the Augustinian-Thomistic consensus. The Westminster Confession defines the providence of God in this way:

> God the great creator of all things doth uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures, actions, and things, from the greatest even to the least, by His most wise and holy providence, according to His infallible foreknowledge, and the free and immutable counsel of His own will, to the praise of the glory of His wisdom, power, justice, goodness, and mercy.[^7]

Thomas C. Oden captures the essence of this definition: “Providence is the expression of the divine will, power, and goodness through which the Creator preserves creatures, cooperates [concurs] with what is coming to pass through their actions [secondary causes], and guides creatures in their long-range purposes.”[^8]

These definitions are structured around the interrelated, three-phase activity of God frequently employed in standard, Reformed treatments of DDP: preservation, cooperation (concursus), and government. The WCF, 5.1, presents providence as the conviction that God, in his goodness and power, *preserves, accompanies, and directs* the entire universe. Some theologians detect this triad in Romans 11:36: “from him” (preservation), “through him” (cooperation/concurrence), and “to him” (government) are all things.[^9]

Over the centuries, the church has wrestled with challenges arising against this doctrine.[^10] Some have conflated God with the world (*pantheism*), and so have undermined the need for providence at all. Some others have held to a *deistic* view of God that affirms creation but not providence. *Dualism* has challenged DDP by positing two opposing forces in constant tension, neither of which can function in any sort of providential manner. *Indeterminism* denies any intelligible control over the world. On the other end of the spectrum, *determinism* and *omnicausality* destroys secondary causation and human freedom. The doctrine of *chance* recognizes the “governance of randomness,” but not of an intelligent and personal cause. And *fatalism* fails to accept the benevolence and sovereign freedom of God, choosing instead to resign all responsibility to an impersonal force, adopting the motto, *Que será será*—“whatever will be, will be.” In response to each of these, the church consistently and with consensus has countered with a defense of the traditional DDP, whose truth has been shown to be based on strong biblical warrant and sound systematics.

DDP remained relatively stable throughout the early and medieval periods, but during the modern period, DDP received intense criticism. One line of critique has come from those that argue it entails God being the “author of evil.” Another strand arose with Schleiermacher, who “hijacked the doctrine of providence, forcing it into his reformulation of the Christian faith as the feeling of absolute dependence on the divine spirit of the world.”[^11] Furthermore, Process Theology attempts to reformulate God’s providence in terms of a *response* to creation, not *government*, which has opened the door to the idea of risk within the notion of divine providence and the “openness” of the future. In response, a number of leading evangelical thinkers have shown these challenges to fail, and have insisted on the integrity and orthodoxy of the traditional formulation of DDP.[^12]

DDP presents us with theological tensions that also create pastoral difficulties. One example is the “problem of prayer.” The Bible’s teaching on both the reality of divine providence and the Christian duty to pray is clear. But if God does indeed reign with sovereignty over this world in providential preservation, cooperation, and governance, and directs all things to the ends which he has determined for them, then why pray? Within a high, meticulous view of providence, prayer seems meaningless, some will argue, since it assumes that we have some role to play in God’s outworking of the future. But if we do have a meaningful role to play in God’s “vast, eternal plan” that Tevye speaks of, then that would seem to mean that God has no definite “plan” in mind.[^13] Such are the kinds of tensions that the pastor must be sensitive to and be prepared to clarify with theological precision. With this in mind, we now turn to consider the pastoral application of DDP and how it can be utilized in pastoral care as a means of comfort and a call to faithfulness through the experience of pain and suffering.

## Pastoral Care

DDP is relevant for the Christian because it is “interwoven with the power and courage to live the responsible life day by day, to persevere through trying details, and to celebrate divine guidance amid hostile environments.”[^14] Thus, DDP can be a very powerful instrument of consolation and discipleship in pastoral care. The pastoral vocation will expose the pastor to the life situations of others that require not only sound theology to address, but also a comforting demeanor and spiritual wisdom. Pastors frequently find themselves providing counsel to those who are in the midst of crisis. The counselee may be having marital problems, problems with children, struggles with anxiety and depression, spiritual oppression, the tragic loss of a loved one, financial problems, addictions, etc., or any combination of these. From my own experience, I find that many who are in the middle of a crisis in their lives usually express their frustration in terms of doubt and confusion—a phenomenon that may be described as the “existential struggle with the problem of evil.” The psalmist frequently tracks in the language of despair and lamentation (e.g. Pss. 22; 13: “How long, O Lord...?”) in the face of hardship and suffering. Like the psalmist, despairing Christians want to know where God is in their own pain and suffering, or that of their family and friends. Some may wonder why God allows them to experience the trial and tragedy of their present situation. Their suffering poses a threat to their faith in the providence of a good and loving God. Herman Bavinck describes the tension:

> There is so much in life that is oppressive and that robs us of the strength to live and act. There are the adversities and disappointments which we meet on life’s way. There are those terrible calamities and disasters which sometimes cause hundreds and thousands of lives to be lost in nameless anguish. But life in its ordinary course also can sometimes raise doubts in the mind about the providence of God.[^15]

Many who seek counsel in their suffering struggle under the load that this tension creates, and may even become bitter against the God who allows them or others they know to suffer. One expression of such feelings about God is expressed in the novel by Fyodor Dostoyevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*. For the character Ivan Karamazov, the problem comes down to the rejection of God’s good providence. After concluding that he would have nothing to do with the Christian God who allowed—even ordained(!)—his suffering, Ivan declares, “I would rather remain with my unavenged suffering and unsatisfied indignation, even if I am wrong.”[^karamazov] Ivan is unequivocally rejecting God on the basis of his experience of seemingly meaningless suffering. Herein lies the danger of misunderstanding and misappropriating DDP. Bavinck continues:

> Is not mystery the portion of all mankind? The worm of restlessness and fear gnaws at all existence. Is it not true that God has a quarrel with His creatures and that we perish in His wrath and are terrified by his anger? No, it is not the unbelievers and the frivolous only, but the children of God also, and these the most deeply of all, who are seized upon by the awful seriousness of reality. And sometimes the question forces its way from the heart up to the lips: Can it be that God created man on the earth for nothing?[^16]

The thought that God would allow tragedy to befall his good world and the creatures he loves seems, *prima facie*, to betray a sinister quality in God’s otherwise perfect character. Nonetheless, only with DDP can rich encouragement and comfort be provided to those in despair (lit., “loss of hope”), and that whether in good times or bad, they can rest assured that God is in complete control, working out his good pleasure through the events that from the human perspective seem so bad and pointless. Of course, this is easier said than done for someone going through trials and experiencing the pain of loss or hardship in their lives. But in due time, when the clouds lift and the tenacious trust in the goodness of God perseveres, the idea of God’s providence that caused the believer to doubt his goodness now becomes the very idea that convinces them of his overwhelmingly gracious, fatherly care. Bavinck notes the transition:

> But then the despondent Christian by a faith in God’s creation and providence again raises his head up high. […] Behind all secondary causes there lurks and works the almighty will of an almighty God and a faithful Father.[^17]

In the work of ministering to a person in the throes of disillusionment and despair, the pastor must exercise great caution and care. When the pain is fresh and raw, the sufferer may just need a loving friend who will hear them out and provide emotional and spiritual comfort through their mere presence and attention. In time, they may be ripe for words of counsel and a reminder of the character of God. This is not the time for theologizing, but of recognizing how theology undergirds the task of pastoral care. It is here where DDP and its bearing on the gospel of Jesus Christ can offer much grace for the hurting one. The Christian God is not a removed and distant Creator who leaves us alone in our suffering, but joins with us in our suffering. The greatest measure of God’s providence is displayed in the wisdom of His Son, Jesus Christ, who humiliated Himself to a cruel death on the cross. The mighty God of the universe stripped Himself of His glory and became shame for the sins of the world, and all this in complete harmony with His providential plan. As pastors, says Harold L. Senkbeil, “Sometimes the only comfort we have to bring is this: We have a suffering God.”[^18] J.P. Moreland and William Lane Craig rightly acknowledge that “paradoxically, even though the problem of evil is the greatest objection to the existence of God, at the end of the day God is the only solution to the problem of evil. God is the final answer to the problem of evil, for he redeems us from evil and takes us into the everlasting joy of an incommensurable good, fellowship with himself.”[^19] And this is only made possible through the gospel of the Lord Jesus Christ. These are the truths that should be communicated tenderly and with great sensitivity in pastoral counseling in order to demonstrate the glorious depth of DDP and its ability to comfort the soul with the power of the gospel. The good news of Christ and His work is the ultimate vindication and demonstration of the goodness of God’s providence and the source of healing for the soul in sorrow.

But DDP is not a pastoral “magic wand.” It is necessary to understand that when applying DDP in pastoral care, a patient process is often involved. In addition to words of counsel offered to help heal a hurting person and shore up his trust in a good and sovereign God, there is often a period of indeterminable time that usually must pass for him to be restored to emotional, physical, and spiritual health. In many cases, people overcome their pain and distress through long periods of prayer and reflection on certain truths, and a remembering of God’s faithfulness in past trials. God providentially works through their minds and hearts and memories as they poise themselves to receive His grace and caring presence.

A classic example of this process can be found in the life and work of John Milton, the famous seventeenth-century poet. During the writing of his *Nineteenth Sonnet*, Milton experienced the degeneration of his physical sight and was threatened with the possibility of complete blindness. Russell M. Hillier argues that the *Nineteenth Sonnet* “comprises a meditation upon divine providentialism.”[^20] The pattern of the Sonnet, he argues, reflects that of an epistle that Milton wrote to the eminent Athenian physician, Leonard Philaras, with whom he consulted in a quest for a cure for his blindness. Milton writes, “Because if, as it is written, ‘Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word proceeding from the mouth of God,’ that is, why should not someone be at rest in this same idea, that it is sufficient to have sight, not in the eyes alone, but in the leadership and providence of God.”[^21] Hillier recognizes that “the sonnet’s scope, like that of Milton’s letter to Philaras, arguably follows a similar pattern, moving from an agitated state to rest, finally, in God’s disposal of events.”[^22] It is through this process of the patient contemplation of God’s providence that Milton comes to the end of his questioning of God and the seeming meaningless evil that he experienced in his failing sight.

>  ...But patience, to prevent  
> That murmur, soon replies, “God doth not need  
>  Either man’s work or his own gifts; who best  
>  Bear his mild yoke, they serve him best. His state  
> Is Kingly. Thousands at his bidding speed  
>  And post o’er Land and Ocean without rest:  
>  They also serve who only stand and wait.[^23]  

It is only through this “patience to prevent murmur” that he becomes spiritually and intellectually satisfied with the confidence and trust he developed in God’s providential power, resting not on his own understanding, but in the all-sufficient power and wisdom of God. This is pastorally instructive, in that it reminds us of the importance of remembering that God’s providential activity is not a mechanical process, but a personal work of love; not merely causal, but relational.[^24] As Michael Horton reminds us, “God doesn’t just act upon creation, but within it...”[^25]

Like Milton, those whom we counsel can be guided to a place where they may come to rest in God’s providence, though it may take a long time and great patience. As pastors, we must continue to affirm the providence of God in pastoral counseling, holding fast to its biblical foundation, its theological merit, and the practical confidence and consolation it gives the Christian, especially in times of pain and suffering.[^26] The sixteenth-century Jesuit theologian, Luis de Molina, captures the consoling power of God’s meticulous providential care of his creation:

> [T]he leaf hanging from the tree does not fall, nor does anything else whatever happen without God’s providence and will either intending it as a particular or permitting it as a particular. *This is the greatest consolation of the righteous*, who place all their hope in God and rest comfortably in the shadow of the wings of His providence, desiring that in both prosperity and adversity God’s will with regard to them might always be fulfilled.[^27]

John Calvin concurs:

> [The Christian’s] comfort, I say, is to know that his heavenly Father so holds all things in his power, so rules by his authority and will, so governs by his wisdom [note the triad!], that nothing can happen except he determine it. Moreover, it comforts him to know that he has been received into God’s safekeeping and entrusted to the care of his angels, and that neither water, nor fire, nor iron can harm him, except in so far as it pleases God as governor to give them occasion . . .[^28]
