The marriage relationship between a husband and his wife is a covenantal union.[^1] This is true of every marriage, whether it is entered into with full knowledge of its obligations or not. Marriage is God's idea, and it is for the world. Herman Bavinck puts it plainly:

> Church and state neither invented nor instituted marriage; they can only validate it before God and before people. Marriage itself is of divine origin; he established the law for marriage, a law that binds every person, as well as the church and the state; he created it as a reflection of that most intimate fellowship that he in Christ enjoys with his church.[^2]

When the integrity of a marriage deteriorates to the point of considering divorce, it creates a complex ethical landscape that demands careful consideration. Pastorally, it presents the leaders of the church with the challenge of determining whether there are valid grounds for dissolving the union.

I propose a covenant-repudiation model for assessing grounds for divorce in extreme cases of marital demise. This model is rooted in the biblical witness to marriage as a covenant, with far-reaching, positive obligations, far beyond simply avoiding "adultery" or "abandonment." These positive provisions include protection, conjugal care in terms of respect, personal allegiance, and intimacy that culminates in regular and exclusive sexual relations (all entailed by the "leaving and cleaving" of Genesis 2:24).

This model I am proposing recognizes that one spouse may so radically and obstinately repudiate these obligations, constitutive of marriage, that the covenantal integrity of the marriage is effectively and judicially breached, constituting grounds for divorce.

This approach navigates between a more narrow approach that restricts biblical warrant for divorce to two isolated exceptions mentioned in the New Testament (Matthew 5:32; 19:9 [The "Matthean Exception"]; 1 Corinthians 7:15 [The "Abandonment Clause"]), and a more liberal approach that loosely and haphazardly defines what constitutes a breach of covenant apart from any serious exegetical engagement with the pertinenet biblical texts, which runs the risk of conflating covenant *violations* with covenant *repudiation*, leaving the identification of grounds for divorce up to a vague and subjective notion of "covenant-breaking."

The propsed model offers theological constraints on the interpretation of the relevant biblical texts, and provides a more robust framework for assessing the covenantal integrity of a marriage—or the demise thereof—in light of the broader biblical witness to marriage as a covenant (Genesis 2:24; Malachi 2:14-16; Ephesians 5:22-33).
