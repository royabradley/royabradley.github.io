> *Parenting by God's Promises: How to Raise Children in the Covenant of Grace*
>
> Joel R. Beeke
>
> Sanford, FL: Reformation Trust, 2011.

Beeke grounds Christian parenting in the covenant of grace. Like Abram, parents live by God's promise, which God establishes and secures in Christ and which they keep by faith and obedience. From the Reformed view of covenant children he draws the duties of bringing children to Christ, teaching them doctrine and shepherding their hearts. He then casts parents as prophets, priests and kings in the home, who teach, intercede and rule in love, with discipline both preventive and corrective. Drawing on the Puritans, he adds practical counsel on piety, listening, speech, siblings and teenagers, all held within a thousand-generation hope.

## Covenantal Foundations for Parenting

### Encouragement Through the Covenant

There can be a lot of discouragement in raising children, but Beeke argues that there is great encouragement in God's covenant. Beeke rehearses the Abrahamic covenant, noting that Abram's request for a sign which would confirm God's promise to him was an act of faith, not doubt—"Faith can have questions," says Beeke (4). The sign God gave Abram was a "cutting" ceremony, a common way of making covenants in Abram's day. This particular ceremony was peculiar, though: Abram laid out the carcasses, but God did not show up, only the birds of prey, which threatened the integrity of the ceremony, and Abram fretted. Beeke likens this to how parents often feel regarding the rearing of their children in the faith—sometimes it seems that God is not working through all *our* work. "Abram's trial of faith is often the trial of God-fearing parents" (6).

After Abram labored all day to fend off the birds of prey, he fell asleep out of his exhaustion; but it was no ordinary sleep—it was a deep sleep. Beeke suggests that it was a "supernatural sleep" (7). In this sleep, Abram experienced the "horror of great darkness" (Gen. 15:12 KJV), which was "a kind of prophecy of suffering to come" (8). But through the emblems of the smoking furnace and the burning lamp that passed through the cut pieces *alone*, God made a covenant oath to be with Abram's seed in their slavery, and to be his salvation. Beeke finds this enormously comforting. The fulfillment of the covenant promise does not depend on Abram. Translated into the realm of parenting, this means that our success in parenting does not depend on us; it is unilaterally *established* by God and secured in Christ, but is *maintained* through our obedience and mediated by grace. This is the classic balance between our responsibility and God's sovereignty. We approach a *giving* God as *receiving* sinners. Biblical parenting is a walk of faith that leans on God's covenant promises. This is the foundation.

### Children of the Covenant

It is sobering to remember that our children are sinners to the core, and that they entered the world with the disease of sin because they inherited it from us, their parents. This recognition should make us more compassionate and understanding toward our children *as sinners*, because we represent God to them, and that is how God treats us in our sin.

God has a claim on our children; they are his covenant children that he has given to us to rear and protect. "Biblical parenting is a solemn responsibility because we are called to treat our children as God would treat them" (16).[^1] And so the question arises: How does God treat them?

Beeke references 1 Corinthians 7:14 and its teaching that the children of believing parents are "sanctified" and "holy." There is some ambiguity to this notion, and so there have been different ways of understanding the exact meaning of these terms as they refer to children of believing parents. Beeke outlines three main approaches to the question: the internal and saving ("presumptive") view, the external, non-covenantal view (baptistic), and the external and covenantal view (held by many Reformed and Presbyterian churches and corroborated by their confessions). Beeke holds to the latter position, and then discusses some of the principles implied therein.

1. Covenant children have a need to be regenerated ("born again"). John 3:7: "Ye must be born again." The home is the primary school of faith.[^2]
2. Saving children of believers is God's *normal* work of adding members to the company of the elect (through time). God delights to save our children, which motivates us to labor to bring them to him.[^3]

These two principles give rise to some clear duties.

1. We bring our children to Christ as the only foundation and perfect sacrifice for their salvation.
2. We must cultivate an affectionate obedience toward God in them (shepherding their hearts).
3. We must teach our children Christian doctrine (Ex. 12:24–27; 2 Tim. 3:14–15).[^4]

Beeke advocates for balancing covenant responsibility and hope: "our view of children should mingle hope and caution" (27). We should operate on the basis of a "cautious optimism" (27) regarding our children's status, yet we should not be too skeptical, forgetting the covenant promises.

### Rearing Children by Faith

Beeke holds up Moses' parents, Amram and Jochebed, as stellar examples of parenting according to God's promises. Like all good parents, they did for Moses what he could not do for himself when he was an infant.[^5] Both Amram and Jochebed are credited for hiding their son, both playing active roles in his protection and nurture. Beeke notes that with commendable parents like this and a sister like Miriam, we can safely assume that the spiritual atmosphere of their home was healthy and rich. They were people of faith.

The context into which Moses was born was tense and difficult. Pharaoh had turned against the sturdy Hebrews in fear and was set on crushing them into submission and death. The hiding of Moses was an act of faith, but we are not sure of any of the details related to how they accomplished this under the circumstances. When Moses could be hid no longer, Jochebed realized she had to find another way to protect her child from danger, not unlike how parents today ask how they can protect their own children from spiritual "Pharaohs" that seek to destroy them. Amram and Jochebed had faith, but they needed to *act* on that faith, which means they had to risk by sending Moses outside of their direct supervision and care. Beeke says that faith in these situations remembers two things: our baptized children of the covenant are under its protection wherever they go, and our children are never too far away that our prayers cannot reach them. By faith, Moses' parents saw Moses' potential in the comeliness of his face, reflecting the beauty of God in him (Acts 7:20) and they saw God's sovereignty in the affairs of men, and so this mighty Pharaoh was no match for the mighty warrior of heaven.

"God's providence in response to faith is amazing," says Beeke. Moses was not only spared his life, but was adopted by Pharaoh's own daughter and nursed/reared by his own mother. This was far beyond what Amram and Jochebed could have imagined. Moses was educated in Pharaoh's courts and rose to prominence, but when his time came to reject Pharaoh's authority and repudiate his status, he acted out of the faith of his parents. And through that act of faith, God used him as a prophet *par excellence* (Ps. 90; Deut. 34:10).

### The Covenant Promise

In this chapter, Beeke exposits Acts 2:39 with regard to three aspects of the promise to which it alludes: its [1] subjects, its [2] benefits, and its [3] responsibilities. Those who disagree about whether infants should be baptized disagree about what this text teaches. Beeke notes that a simple word study on the term "baptism" in Scripture will not yield a clear doctrine of the sacrament. He says we must understand the nature of a sacrament as an outward sign of an inward reality to appreciate the doctrine of infant (covenant) baptism. Just as the bloody sacrifice of Passover was replaced by the unbloody meal of the Lord's Supper, so the bloody sacrifice of circumcision is replaced by baptism. Paul even calls baptism "the circumcision of Christ" (Col. 2:11). The sacraments only have significance when understood in terms of the covenant.

Additionally, there is a fundamental unity between the two Testaments of Scripture.[^6] The Jews in the NT era were accustomed to thinking in terms of promise → fulfillment, so when Peter preached repentance, he reminded them that it was in the context of the ancient covenant promise which is "to you, and to your children" (Acts 2:39). The scope of the covenant had always included the children of believers, and this was taken for granted by the Jews in Peter's day, otherwise there would have been a riot, or at least a lot of questions, and not 3,000+ baptisms! Covenant children are the proper subjects of baptism, "because, according to God's Word, they are included in the covenant and church of God" (45).

There are many benefits to this promise. Covenant children have the Word of God taught to them, they are brought up in the church, the nurturing mother, may attend schools where the Word of God is central. They are saturated with the Word, and this is a great benefit. Secondly, God regards covenant children differently; they have a special status in Him. God promises to provide, protect, and prosper them, and to exercise His mercy toward them, so long as they trust in Him.

The covenant promise also comes with great responsibilities. The covenant is one-sided in its *establishment*, but two-sided in its *maintenance*. And so the first responsibility of a covenant member is to "repent, turn from sin, cut away sin, and thus circumcise the foreskins of our hearts" (49). The second responsibility is that we take refuge (trust) in the blood of the covenant, the blood of Jesus Christ. Covenant maintenance is the believer's responsibility: "The just shall live by faith" (Rom. 1:17). The covenant promise is of great encouragement to parents, because their daily labors in training their children in godliness is backed up by God's sure Word in the sum of the whole covenant: Jesus Christ. He is faithful.

### Bringing Children to Christ

We cannot give our children what they need most—new hearts, but we can bring them to Christ.[^7] There are a number of ways that we can fail to do so, however. We, ourselves can avoid going to Jesus, so we will not be able to bring our children with us. We can fail by not speaking of Christ to our children—of his glory, of his love for sinners, of his power to save, and of his willingness to receive those who come to him in faith. We can be hypocritical, confessing our allegiance to Christ, but living contrary to his commands. We can fail to love the church, Christ's bride. We can fail to trust God's promises. We can be apathetic to spiritual things in general. In all these ways, parents can hinder their children from coming to the Lord.

Beeke uses Mark 10:13–16 to explore the various aspects of the responsibilities of parents in bringing their children to Christ. This begins with prayer, and it happens at every age. We are to keep on bringing our children to Christ *despite opposition*. Beeke offers ten practical ways to bring children to Jesus, making it clear that these things in themselves are no guarantee that fruit will be borne; that, of course, is the Spirit's work, and so we do these things *in faith*. Beeke says that in bringing their children to Christ, parents must:

1. Be fully convinced that our children need Jesus and his salvation.
2. Realize that our children belong to the Lord in three ways: he is their *Creator*, their heavenly *Benefactor*, and their covenant *Redeemer*.
3. Surrender our children to the Lord.
4. Speak to and live with our children in a Christ-centered way.
5. Strive to make a godly impression on our children with our lives.[^8]
6. Show our children our reverence and joy toward God.
7. Teach our children the whole counsel of God, both law and gospel.
8. Offer our children Christ-centered views of current events.
9. Lovingly warn our children about being outside of Christ.
10. Become spiritual mentors for our grandchildren.

Mark 10:14b-15 indicates that we can learn much from our children. Becoming like children, in the sense that Jesus is employing it here, refers to a child's "capacity for unreserved, wholehearted, unwavering faith in the people they love" (60).

As we bring our children to Christ, "we are to be urgent, but never desperate" (61). Jesus blessed the children who were brought to him, in three ways: he took them up (he embraced them), he put his hands upon them (laying on of hands, a gesture of favor and acceptance), and he blessed them. This blessing is essential to our task as covenant nurturers. And with that blessing, we now turn to the responsibilities we bear in the domestic offices to which we have been commissioned as parents of covenant children.

## Parenting as Prophets, Priests, and Kings

### Our Responsibility as Parents

Parenting is a great and sober responsibility.[^9] God wants godly seed, and so He gives his children to parents for nurture and cultivation. Beeke surveys three faulty parenting models at play in North American society at large (though they vary some across cultures), and that are even present among Reformed Christians, and then offers a fourth alternative. Noting these types of parenting can help avoid slipping into them. Some parent as [1] "consensus administrators," running their households according to a majority vote. Some parents function as [2] "horns of plenty," showering their children with money and gifts, thinking that they will substitute for love and loyalty. Other parents are [3] "wishing wells," parenting primarily from their feelings, not principles. A fourth, biblical model of parenting understands parents as analogical [4] "mediators" through Christ's offices of prophet, priest, and king. All Christians, by virtue of their union with Christ, share in these offices in their status as members of His body, and particularly in their parenting role.

The essential function of a prophet is not *fore*-telling, but *forth*-telling—being a spokesman for God. As prophets to our children, we tell them God's truth as revealed in the Bible. Prophets are to tell the truth with warmth and passion, in an act of duty and love. Likewise, our prophetic ministry to our children must be accompanied by warmth and passion. Parents, as priests, have access to God, and so they represent Christ to their children as representatives and instruments of his blessings to them. As kings, parents shepherd their children from a position of sacrificial authority.

### Teaching our Children as Prophets

The prophetic ministry of parents involves teaching children what they are to believe concerning God, and what God requires of them. Ephesians 6:4 explains how this work should be approached, summed up in the term *paideia*, which has in view the training of all the parts of a child's being. We are not neutral prophets who make suggestions to our children regarding how we think they should live. As God's prophets in the home, we are to command our children to follow the way of God. Beeke focuses on two ways parents accomplish this responsibility: catechizing and godly modeling.

We are to catechize our children in Christian doctrine through the Scriptures themselves, especially the Psalms, historic (age-appropriate) catechisms, family worship, giving our children opportunities to articulate their faith in clear terms and to pray aloud with confidence and fluency. The rewards of consistency and diligence in this are sweet, and with the blessing of Christ, will produce abundant harvest in our children's souls.

Children learn from what we tell them, but they learn even more from what we show them, so we must model for them what we want them to display in their own lives. Beeke says that parents are the most important books their children will ever read. Sharing our lives with our children should be done organically and should be normalized in the life of the family. Parents must work as a team in this ministry, which means they must communicate with one another, and must present a unified front in the things that matter most.

### What Should We Teach our Children?

The father of Proverbs clearly takes seriously his role as a parent-prophet (cf. Prov. 3:1–6). He insists that his son remember the law of God, mercy and truth, and faith in God. Beeke says these items cannot be emphasized too much in the life of a child. Beeke offers ten foundation truths that parents should "prophesy" to their children.

1. *Who God is and what He is like*. Children must be taught the character and attributes of God, which leads them to an understanding of the next truth.
2. *The seriousness of sin*. A recognition of their own sin is one of the most important things for children to learn about themselves, and parents must work to awaken that awareness.
3. *Hell*. No one is too young to taste death, and so children are never too young to think about what will happen when they die. We should not speak casually about the reality of hell; it is a serious and sober reality. Beeke says there are two extremes to avoid: an overly graphic or cartoonish approach versus an avoidance of the subject altogether. We should talk more about heaven than hell (the Bible does), but we should not avoid teaching our children about the reality of hell and its terror.
4. *Moral law*. The Ten Commandments should be the core of this teaching for children, the roots of which run deep into the heart. We should illustrate how this is the case through skillful illustration. This will reinforce their desperate need for a savior and redeemer in Jesus Christ.
5. *Necessity of the new birth*. Children, like the rest of us, need new hearts, and so parents are not simply behavior management coaches, but rather are shepherds who lead their children to their Savior. Outward obedience is not sufficient; they need to be re-born/re-created, and we must treat their misbehavior as a matter of the heart.
6. *The Lord Jesus Christ*. Painting a portrait of Jesus to our children is a way to help them dwell on the beauty of his person as a cause for reflection and worship.
7. *The blood of Christ*. Our children need to know that Jesus' atoning blood is the only source of their salvation. They need to understand that no amount of human effort can accomplish what God has accomplished in Christ.
8. *The necessity of faith in Christ*. Children need to be shown that faith in the blood of Jesus is their salvation.
9. *Sanctification and holiness*. Children must learn to walk obediently before the Lord, growing in maturity and holiness.
10. *The joys of heaven*. We should set before our children the beauty and bliss of a world without sin, without death, without pain, and that is everlasting in the presence of Christ, so that they might come to love and long for that estate and live unto it.

### Training in Godly Living

Beeke says we should train (nurture) our children in three key areas: intellectual, social, and physical. The intellectual training of children includes teaching them, not just the facts about the Bible, but to think biblically about everything. Parents also should be engaged in their children's work at school. There should be a system in place to manage children's use of modern media and a proactive strategy to get good reading material into their hands. Children should be engaged by their parents in discussion about the movies they watch, books they read, music they listen to, current events, or how they would react in certain circumstances. Parents should support their children's teachers with prayer and model respect for them as gifts of God. Parents should also support their children's pastors, elders, and church leadership. Parents should not pit one child's intellectual ability against another's—children will not forget such remarks. Whatever intellectual ability our children have, they need to recognize that they are "debtors to God for every ability, every strength, every asset they possess" (1 Cor. 4:7).

The social training of children is a matter of "adorn[ing] the doctrine of God our Savior" (Tit. 2:10) with proper etiquette, social grace, and decorum. Our children should be taught to show respect to everyone. These are basic ways to express love for neighbors. We should give them a taste for fine art, literature, and music. Cultural awareness is an important part of child training.

The physical training of children is entailed by a love for the body as a gift from God. Sleep, a healthy diet, and plenty of exercise are all components of this. Sexuality and sexual boundaries should be discussed. We should teach them balance in the realm of competitive sports, as our culture has idolized them ("sports mania" [109]), and we must teach balance with respect to dress and appearance (modesty).

Beeke concludes with four practical principles:

1. Use the word of God and depend on the Holy Spirit.
2. Use every opportunity.
3. Do not abandon your post.
4. It is never too late to start.

### Serving as Priests in the Home

Like priests, we are to "present our children to God as sacrifices sanctified by the Spirit through the gospel (Rom. 15:16)" (113). In other words, God blesses our children through us. Job exercised a priestly role "continually" for his children (Job 1:1–2, 5). Job is a model for parent-priests in the way he [1] sanctified his children, [2] offered sacrifices for them, [3] and he persevered in prayer for them.

Job sanctified his children by setting them apart by calling them to worship. Then he exhorted them to prepare for the approaching sacrifice he was to make on their behalf, and instructing them in the way of gratitude. His motivation for all this was rooted in his fear of God (Job 1:1, 8).

On this side of the cross, there is no more offering for sin, but parents can still offer the sacrifice of intercessory prayer "continually." This intercession has no power to save our children from their sin, and our children cannot save themselves, but we plead Christ's witness in their baptism that he has promised that our children are inheritors of the covenant promise along with us. We bring our children to Christ every day in prayer.

### Sympathizing with Our Children

Parent-priests should have pity on their children "for their infirmities and general neediness as human beings, as children, and as fallen offspring of Adam, who are vulnerable to temptation and sin" (124). Beeke distinguishes sympathy from self-pity, the former being a work of the Spirit, the latter being a work of the flesh (2 Cor. 7:10). Beeke uses the example of Jesus' pity on Peter following his three-fold denial and on the other disciples who forsook him in his darkest hour. Jesus struck a delicate balance between exercising priestly sympathy without excusing sin. As the rooster crowed, Jesus looked across the hall of Caiaphas, and when Peter saw his face, Peter melted in shame; but subsequent events revealed that Jesus' response was one of compassion and forgiveness at Peter's brokenness.[^10]

Beeke notes that Peter was a three-dimensional character: often being found as a man of *firsts* in the Gospel narratives, he was humbled and placed *last* in his sin of denial, and once restored by the sympathy and grace of Christ, he was once again securely placed *inside* the inner circle of disciples. This is the kind of priestly ministry parents are to exercise with their children in the home.

### Exercising Loving Rule as Kings

Parents are Christ's representative rulers in the home. Beeke suggests that Joshua is perhaps the best model of this in Scripture. Joshua took charge of his household, making the declaration, "We *will* serve the Lord" (Josh. 24:15). Children will often rebel against this leadership in one way or another, but they should be taught that disobedience to parents is a serious sin against God and his design for the household economy. Exercising loving rule as kings in the home requires, well, both *love* and *rule*—one without the other is dangerous and not effective. Loving rule entails discipline, both preventative and corrective. The Bible gives us a clear mandate regarding parental discipline (with careful words and "the rod"), a clear goal for such discipline (to teach our children to obey God as the ultimate authority, and the authorities that flow down from his)[^11], and a clear means of discipline (teaching and instructing, nurturing and admonishing [preventative]; verbal reproof and corporal punishment [corrective]). Beeke notes a divine pattern in God's corrective discipline: verbal reproof, then corporal punishment. As children grow older, the emphasis leans more and more toward verbal reproof. Administering proper discipline in all its various forms is an act of loving, kingly rule.

### Preventative Discipline

Paul's instruction to fathers in Ephesians 6:4 begins with a negative command (injunction against provocation to wrath) and ends with a positive command (direction to nurture and admonish). In this same vein, Beeke offers several ways this instruction can be made concrete in the home as parents exercise their kingly rule as God's representatives.

Parents must conquer the will of their children at a young age, instilling in them a disposition to obey, recognizing that conquering their will is not the same thing as crushing their spirit. Parents should also provide clear rules, minimizing vagueness in their expectations for obedience. [1] "Clean up your room" and [2] "Pick up everything on the floor and put it away" demonstrate the difference. When it comes time to evaluate the child's performance, obedience can be measured well in terms of [2], but could be left open to interpretation with [1]. Parents also must walk with integrity (not moral perfection) in all areas of their lives. Children need to see consistency in the practices of their parents, which demonstrates a sincerity in their faith and instills confidence in a child, knowing his parents are not being whimsical in their expectations. Parents should also be in the habit of asking questions of their children to stimulate thought and conversation, further deepening a relationship of trust and camaraderie. Finally, parents should lavish love upon their children so that there will be no doubt in their mind who loves them the most.

### Corrective Discipline

Beeke notes that the administration of corrective discipline "is the most disagreeable aspect of exercising discipline in the home" (153). Nevertheless, it is a necessary component of our duty to bring our children up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord, for He, too, disciplines his children so that they might mature into godliness. Beeke says that our corrective discipline should be accompanied by a number of characteristics if it is to be done well (biblically, effectively).

It must be done in God's name. It is easy for parents to forget that they are God's ministers of discipline in the home, but keeping our focus on our role as representatives of God, particularly in punishing wrongdoing as a form of training, has at least two benefits: it keeps parents from being indifferent about their responsibility to discipline their children and it keeps parents from carelessness about the task.

Parents should administer corrective discipline in love. There is a personal cost in investing ourselves in disciplining our children, and we should learn to see this sacrifice as a matter of selfless affection toward our children. Beeke marks a few errors to avoid here. We should not think that we can love our children without administering the rod of correction, nor should we think that we can correct them without love, solely on the basis of our whimsical emotions in the moment.

Parents should administer punishment with prayer. Husband and wife must pray daily for God's wisdom in the discipline of their children, remembering that God's wisdom is "not merely helpful information, but holy inclinations" (156). Parents must also pray that their discipline would bear godly fruit. And parents should never administer corporal punishment without finishing with prayer in the presence of the child.[^12]

- Parents should administer punishment with faith, which gives parents "persevering hope to wait upon the Lord" (157).
- Parents should administer punishment to encourage repentance (Rev. 3:19). The goal of our discipline is not to administer the just penalty—that would be hell. Our goal is to move a child to repentance—it is educational and reformative.
- Parents should administer punishment as a means of training and nurture, not as a means of punitive or retributive justice, like the State. Parents have not been given judicial power over their children, but a shepherding responsibility.
- Punishment should include a teaching element, explaining the "why" according to the child's level of maturity.
- Punishment should be administered according to appropriate degrees. Parents must take into account the child's age, his capacity for understanding, the nature of the offense, and the circumstances in which it occurred. Parents need to be equitable in exercising the rod in order to instill trust and confidence in the offending child that the discipline they are receiving is not arbitrary, but measured.
- Corrective discipline must be administered consistently. Keeping the long-term goals of discipline in mind (e.g., maturity) will help with this.

Beeke then addresses three practical questions that arise from these principles:

1. *How often should we spank children?* This depends on the character of each child, as well as their particular circumstances. The law of diminishing returns applies here. If a child is needing frequent spankings, it may be that other circumstantial matters need to be addressed as well.
2. *On what part of the body should discipline be administered?* The rear end is anatomically suited to receive a spanking, and so it is the best place to give it.
3. *At what age should physical discipline cease?* Common sense is helpful here. When a child has grown so large that it is comical to bend him over the knee for a spanking, for example, it's probably time to find other ways to administer corrective discipline.

Disciplining children in love is the parents' kingly responsibility in the home. Most children will respond positively to the approach to discipline discussed above, and in fact, it will often be the instrument of bringing parent and child into closer relationship with one another. Parents must keep their responsibility to administer discipline within the rubric of God's promises to bless that work.

## Practical Steps for Child-Rearing

### Help From our Puritan Forebears

Taking their cues from the Bible, the Puritans celebrated marriage (including the license for sexual pleasure within that bond), the fruit of the sexual union in the form of children, and large, well-ordered families, all as a way of glorifying God and building his church. The stereotype of Puritan men being "hard, cold, baby-producing machines" is simply not true (171). Puritan husbands were typically tender and careful in their domestic duties. The Puritan approach to discipline involved strong love and strong discipline, an approach advocated in this book by Beeke. Child-rearing was not simply a matter of the family; it was viewed as means by which children "would be well-trained, educated, self-disciplined, cultured, and godly citizens of the commonwealth and stalwart members of Christ's church" (171). In other words, the Puritans "viewed families as nurseries for the church and society" (171).

Puritan husbands and wives typically worked as a team, practicing headship and submission with mutual respect and love. A husband could defer to his wife's desires and skills, delegating to her certain tasks and responsibilities accordingly, and she was allowed to admonish and correct her husband privately.

The Puritans had much to say regarding the upbringing of children in a wide variety of situations. During pregnancy, there was a dual duty to pray daily for the health of mother and child, and to protect them from potential harm (given that the mortality rate in pregnancy and childbirth was so high). After birth, Puritan mothers practiced breastfeeding as a means of both nutrition and as a bonding practice between mother and child, which strengthened the bonds within the family at large. They practiced covenantal infant baptism, and assumed their covenantal responsibilities to steward their children's lives on God's behalf (though there were some notable exceptions, e.g., John Bunyan). The Puritans also understood their children needed to be regenerate, and so evangelized their children in covenant nurture. Puritan parents were committed to the religious education of their children, particularly through Bible and catechism training, and by example, all for the goal of godly living.[^13] The Puritans practiced regular family worship as one of the primary means of covenant nurture, which, according to the *Directory for Family-Worship* (1647) typically included prayer, the singing of praise, reading the Bible, teaching the basic truths of Christianity (catechizing), and spiritual conference or discussion. The Puritans were committed to disciplining their children, seeking a balance between strictness and leniency. As they grew older, children were expected to seek parental counsel in making life decisions, including career, spouse, finances, etc.

In Beeke's view, the Puritans serve as excellent examples of covenantal parenting. For them, "the home was the backbone of the church and the commonwealth" (179): "ruin families and ruin all" (Increase Mather).

### Teaching Children Piety

Instilling the virtue of godliness in children is the goal of Christian parenting, as godliness is the practical goal of all Christian living (1 Tim. 4:8). "Piety" (from the Latin, *pietas*) is a synonym for "godliness"; Beeke prefers to use "piety" because it is closer to the "*pietas*" that Calvin and the Puritans were so fond of using. The notion to which the term refers has to do with a certain attitude toward God.[^14] "Godly" or "pious" is used to translate the Hebrew word *ḥasid*, which itself is derived from *ḥesed*, which is translated as the steadfast "lovingkindness" and "everlasting mercy" that God shows His people in accordance with the covenant bond he has made with them. So, because we have received God's *ḥesed*, we are to return to Him our wholehearted love and unswerving devotion, so that we become the *ḥasidim*—the "godly ones."[^15] In the NT, this same idea is communicated by *eusebeia*, the root of which is *sebo*, which means "to worship" or to "reverence." The prefix *eu* ("good") qualifies this idea by showing it to be a matter of the heart and that which is good and pleasing to God. *Eusebeia* is a posture of the heart that expresses itself in the form of godly character.

As surprising as it may be, some object to teaching children piety. Beeke responds to two objections, then offers three motivations for teaching children to be pious.

**Objections**

1. *We don't want our children to become self-righteous Pharisees*. This assumes a negative view of piety, defined by a "holier-than-thou" attitude. True piety is actually the best protection against such an attitude.
2. *All we need to know is the Lord Jesus Christ, and him crucified (1 Cor. 2:2)—the Spirit does the rest*. Knowing the Lord Jesus Christ, and him crucified is comprehensive, not restrictive. There is the objective work of Christ *and* its subjective work in us.

**Motivations**

1. It is a Scriptural command to do so (Eph. 6:4).
2. The biblical saints modeled this project.
3. All the elements of piety are commanded in the law of God.

Beeke turns to reviewing why the Puritans taught their children piety, and then outlines their method.

**Why the Puritans Taught Piety to their Children**[^16]

1. Piety is the best thing to be taught. If we fail here, we fail everywhere.
2. Piety needs to be taught because children are born without it. Holiness does not come naturally, so it needs to be taught.
3. Teaching piety is part of the office of parenting; parents are best suited to the work.
4. Our children belong to God, and his claim on them demands piety.
5. Teaching piety offers a double bond with our children: there is a natural bond through their birth to us, but a second bond can occur when we see them become spiritually born, which unites us to them spiritually.
6. Piety is the most helpful thing we can leave with our children, not money, possessions, or recognition.
7. Teaching piety is a special means for propagating the faith to future generations.

The Puritans followed several principles in their training. They grounded their children in God's word, making it a living part of their daily lives. They had their children read God's word for themselves. They led their children in regular family worship, catechizing, and the discussion of spiritual matters. They seized opportunities for teaching. Gouge identifies three kinds of special opportunities to appropriating God's word to the lives of our children: [1] draw examples from nature and daily life, [2] make use of the means of grace dispensed in public worship, and [3] draw lessons from God's "great and admirable" work, by which Gouge meant God's great acts in history, in our own lives, the great men and women of Christian history, and the great events. Additionally, parents should offer their children a Christian education at the feet of Christian teachers. Finally, parents should show their children piety by parental example through [1] how they live, [2] how they talk, and [3] how they pray.

Beeke concludes this chapter on teaching piety with instruction on how *not* to teach piety and make "a shipwreck in our parental efforts in teaching piety" (192). This path includes focusing on temporal things, giving children a profane example in our lack of piety, treating Christian education carelessly and uncritically, ignoring the basics (the Ten Commandments, the Lord's Prayer, the Apostles' Creed, and the way of salvation in Christ), ignoring daily events in the lives of our children, and shunning all expressions of personal piety, treating religion as if it is merely external.

### Teaching Children to Listen

"Good listeners are commended in Scripture" (195), but listening well does not come naturally to most of us, so it is a virtue that must be taught. Self-absorbed pride is usually the biggest obstacle to good listening; another is the tendency to disregard or dismiss what we do not embrace.[^17] Good listening is related to wisdom, and so we must inculcate this virtue in our children in order that they will become "unselfish listeners and effective communicators [...] in the church, at home, and abroad in society" (196).

Teaching children to listen at church, particularly the sermon, is of prime importance. To do this well, there must be preparation through prayer and proper planning; it will not happen haphazardly.[^18] As the sermon is preached, children should be taught to listen with understanding, like the fourth type of soil in Jesus' parable that receives the seed and nourishes it, producing fruit. Children need to understand that they are not passive participants but active players in the sermon event. They must also listen with faith and humble self-examination.[^19] Finally, we must teach children to put God's Word into practice, for we must be "doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving [our] own selves" (Jas. 1:22).

But good listening is not just for sermons, it is for all of life. Listening is especially helpful in receiving parental training. We must be good listeners ourselves, so that we can teach by example. We also need to spend time together as a family, listening to one another, sharing life together. We also should strive to be honest in our listening, not pretending to listen (disinterestedly), but really listening as an act of love. Parents should initiate conversation through open-ended questions that encourage dialogue, not just pat answers.

Communication is an art, and Beeke offers seven principles for growing in this art and teaching it to children.

1. We should draw out the thoughts of others. Communication is a dialogue, not a monologue.
2. We should let our conversations be ruled by the wisdom of Scripture, speaking of things as the Scriptures speak of them.
3. We should use discernment in what we communicate, not overwhelming our children with too much talk.
4. We should speak respectfully.
5. We should grow in genuine interest and warmth in our love for our children through words.
6. We should show gratitude for the things they do. Gratitude should permeate everything in the life of the family.
7. We should make eye-contact.[^20]

And finally, parents should make listening fun by engaging them in creative ways through reading books, body language other than mere eye contact, repetition games, etc. Listening enhances communication, which in turn enhances relationship. We all want to be heard, and God does, too.

### Taming Children's Tongues

The gift of speech separates man from beast and links us with God and his angels. But it is a gift that is easily abused, and the abuse of speech is as destructive as fire (Jas. 3:5–6). Corrupted speech is detected immediately after the Fall of man in the Garden (Gen. 3). We must teach our children to tame their tongues, for "control of the tongue is a hallmark of true godliness (Ps. 15; Ps. 37:30)" (208). This work of ours depends on the Spirit's work in us to mortify the sin of wicked speech.

The Bible provides clear instruction on the types of speech we should avoid: judgmental or critical words; profanity, obscenity, and vulgarity; outbursts of anger; nagging, murmuring, and complaining. Alternatively, the Bible also gives us instruction on how to use speech for God's glory in the form of: reminding ourselves of what we deserve (death and hell, yet we have life and heaven in Christ); speaking positively to our spouses as a model of godly speech for our children; stressing the positives to our children, not just the negatives; being cheerful in our speech and outlook.

Beeke explores three case studies to illustrate how to train children to use their tongues to honor God and concludes with an encouragement to parents, using Jesus' Upper Room Discourse (John 13–17) as a supreme example. "By word and deed, He poured out His love on the disciples. He said hard things to them, but not harshly. He rebuked and corrected with gentleness. He affirmed His love for them over and over. He ended by taking them to God's throne in prayer, commending them to the Father's love: 'Having loved his own which were in the world, he loved them unto the end' (John 13:1b)" (219).

### Handling Sibling Relationships

Beeke notes that sibling relationships are often overlooked in treatments of the Christian family, but the Bible often speaks about sibling relationships. Many biblical examples are negative, beginning with Cain and Abel (Gen. 4:8), but also details some positive examples (e.g., the reconciliation of Jacob and Esau, Gen. 33:1–7). In the NT, the subject of *philadelphia* ("brotherly love") comes into the foreground, as the church is conceived of as a big, international family or household (1 Tim. 3:15). Because conflicts between siblings can wreck not only individual households, but also the household of Christ, Beeke says that we must prepare our children for adult membership in the church by teaching them the "unfeigned love of the brethren" (1 Pet. 1:22).

This work rests on some foundational principles for sibling relationships. First, a recognition that boys and girls will be different, and this is by God's design, so it is not bad in itself. The characteristics of each often come in conflict with each other, and we must be keen on recognizing the different "languages" of each so we can seek to live in harmony despite the differences. Also, we must recognize that favoritism will yield bitter results. Children often *perceive* favoritism where it is actually not at play because of their own personal sensitivities, but parents should be careful to be equitable (not identical) in their treatment of each child. Finally, we should remember that sinners *will* fight. Sibling conflict should not surprise us; it is baked into the cake, as it were. Children fight because they strive for independence, power, and to be first. We should teach them that these strivings reveal their sinful inclinations.

In order to develop healthy sibling relationships within this *status quo*, parents must teach their children the gospel of Jesus Christ, which is the gospel of peace and reconciliation (Rom. 10:15; Eph. 6:15). This is the starting point. And then practically, we should train our children to worship with each other, to love each other tangibly, to teach and learn from each other ("iron sharpens iron"), and to enjoy each other. Children should be frequently reminded that "their brothers and sisters are lifelong companions,....that their families hold some of the most important and lasting friends they will ever have" (231). Beeke says that this work requires patience as children move from immaturity to maturity in this area: "Our best approach is to keep on parenting, knowing that in time, and by God's grace, our efforts will bear good fruit" (231).

## Practical Helps for Teenagers

### Helping Teenagers Discern God's Will

Beeke offers here a framework for older children (those on the cusp of leaving the home) for making good decisions. The starting point is a desire to discover the will of God for personal life decisions. Beeke gives four reasons why this is important. First, the will of God gives meaning to life that would otherwise be hapless and insignificant. Second, the will of God gives us purpose in living: to glorify God and to enjoy him forever (WSC, Q. 1). Third, discerning God's will is the secret of true happiness in life. And fourth, because God has revealed his will for mankind, there is a seriousness to life; living counts and wrong choices cannot be undone.

God has provided a number of sources of guidance to help us discern His will. Parents should guide their older teenagers in considering these sources as they answer questions about their personal spiritual lives, occupations, and prospective spouses.

The best thing parents can give their children is their own love for the Bible, since it is the ultimate source of God's will. The Bible guides us in spiritual self-examination and allows us to test our interest in godliness and gauge our commitment to God's will. Also, the Scriptures provide principles upon which decisions about occupation can be made, such as the need to prepare wisely and diligently for the future. Furthermore, the Bible gives important guidelines that expose the characteristics one should look for in a mate and the purpose in marriage.

Another source for discerning God's will is His providence—God's governance of all things. Through providence, God teaches us about ourselves through opportunities gained and lost, difficult circumstances or happy successes. Teens should be taught to prayerfully deal with these various circumstances, seeking to understand themselves better through them. We should teach them to pursue matters in accordance with biblical ethics while at the same time trusting in God's providence to direct the way.

Teens should learn to use their gifts and desires to know God's will. They should become self-aware of what natural gifts and desires God has granted them, and seek to use those in their vocation and in their choice of a spouse, in obedience to the Bible.

We should encourage our older teenagers to seek counsel from those who are qualified in wisdom to give sound advice. They must choose spiritual counselors wisely.[^21]

Prayer must be upheld as a constant and consistent practice and source of discerning God's will. Beeke says that we cannot place too much emphasis on the role of prayer in the lives of our children. "Above all, we need to teach our children to pray, 'God, may my life be Thy instrument so that Thy name will be honored as holy, Thy kingdom will come, and people will be brought to obey Thy will as it is obeyed in heaven'" (245).

Discerning God's will does not operate according to a mathematical method. It is a natural consequence of a life lived in faith, obedience, and patience.

### Helping Teenagers Resist Negative Peer Pressure

Beeke uses two biblical cases to illustrate resistance to peer pressure, one negative (Peter withdrawing from table fellowship with Gentiles, about which Paul confronted him [Gal. 2:11–13]) and the other positive (Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-Nego resisting the royal command to worship an idol [Dan. 3]). Parents must help their teenagers stand up to peer pressure with faith and courage.

To do this, Beeke says teenagers must have deep roots in their faith, which must be cultivated by parents. This entails that parents must be living examples of courageous integrity before their children. They also must reinforce biblical concepts with concrete action by showing God to be the great object of their love and desire, by submitting to God's Word as the supreme authority and standard for faith and conduct, by teaching the moral purity of faithfulness in the marriage relationship, and by teaching the sovereignty of God. Parents should also train teenagers in daily Bible reading, meditation, and prayer, and rehearse resistance to peer pressure so that it becomes habitual.

Beeke then offers some "battlefield tactics" to resist peer pressure. Every Christian, especially impressionable teenagers, are engaged in a "fierce and dangerous spiritual war (Eph. 6:12)" (253). One of the tactics of the enemy is ungodly peer pressure.[^22] We must teach our teenagers to stand against and fight the enemy on this front with courage and resolve. To do this, we must help our teenagers understand the strength of temptation, and the necessity to retreat from it. Of course, we should also teach our children to search, love, and know the Scriptures well as a source of defense against peer pressure. We should teach teenagers to lean hard on Christ, who "is our great Captain in our spiritual warfare" (254). They should cling to God as their glorious portion and their fear, as they also lead their friends toward righteousness, discerning who their true friends really are. They should learn to measure each choice by its long-term consequences and find life's purpose in service, not selfishness.

### Helping Teenagers Manage Anger

Beeke says that parents can help their teenagers prevent anger by simply loving them in consistent, tangible ways, by lingering around our teens, showing interest in them as we seek their company, listening to them with interest, lavishing them with our affections, giving them clear and reasonable boundaries, legitimizing an appeals process whereby they may respectfully disagree, and finally, parents should locate the problem of anger in the heart, not the circumstances. When our teens get angry at us, we must not respond in kind (i.e., with anger toward them), but must exercise self-control in keeping with the fruit of the Spirit. As we lead teens to put away sinful anger in their hearts, we must help them understand the roots of their anger, and this requires careful listening on our part as they express their anger in words. Also, we must train them to distinguish righteous and unrighteous anger, to express their anger properly, to pursue biblical peacemaking, taking the lead in making peace, to cultivate self-control, to understand their need for the Holy Spirit's help, to pray with them about their anger issue, to remember that every child is different and expresses and responds to anger differently, to not take our teen's anger personally or try to teach them when they are angry, and to look for logs in our own eyes as we evaluate their anger.

## Conclusion

Beeke reminds parents that they should have and keep a "thousand-generation" vision of parenting, where we see ourselves planting our children like seeds in soil that will grow into an oak that will endure for centuries. He discusses three final stages of parenting that will contribute to leaving a lasting legacy of faith in our families. We must help prepare our children for marriage, since so much promise is rooted in the health and integrity of their marriage. Second, we must prepare our children to leave home well. And finally, as the family grows into the third generation, we must be committed to serving our grandchildren by taking initiative in our relationship with them, coming alongside their parents to help nurture them.
